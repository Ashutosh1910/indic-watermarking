Zen and the Art of the Law of Gravity only came into being in 1687

Stacey Richter

Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance

Somewhere near the beginning of Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, the author, Robert Pirsig, mentions Newton’s Law of Gravity. The author asks (rhetorically it turns out) whether this law of gravity always existed, or whether it was born in that eureka moment when Newton sat under an apple tree in 1687.

Of course it always existed.

This was my first reaction.

It took 200 pages for me to understand why I was totally wrong.

Hang with me here, and I can save you 200 pages of reading… or I can cause you 200 pages of reading depending on how you roll.

The author and his kid on their motorcycle.

In the book, there’s a winding narrative about, loosely, motorcycle maintenance that is sly and artful and touching. But here’s a short, blunt, path to one of the places they linger along their journey — Why the Law of Gravity came into being only when Newton wrote it out.

Let’s say Betty is trying to flip heads ten coin tosses in a row. She manages to do it. Hooray! “How did you do it?” everybody wants to know.

“Well,” says Betty, “right before every one of those ten tosses, I tapped the table twice. Also, I was wearing my Philadelphia Eagles jersey the whole time.” Betty goes on to conclude that to flip heads, one must tap the table twice and wear an Eagles jersey. She calls this “The Law of Flipping Heads.”

Question — Did the Law of Flipping Heads exist before Betty made it up?

No, you will probably say. If I ask you why you think that, I expect you’ll answer, “because it’s wrong.”

You might continue to explain that anyone who tries will flip heads 10 times in a row one out of every 1024 tries. It’s math, baby, and Betty got lucky. You will say this so-called “law” (you’ll make air quotes to emphasize your point) only exists and only came into existence when Betty made it up. It doesn’t describe an actual phenomenon in the known universe.

Fair enough.

But hopefully you see that any law, or abstract concept, that we humans create is an interpretation of tangible phenomenon or observations. In fact, every single day we are bombarded by tangible phenomenon and observations. Why do we decide to cherry pick some of them and string them together into laws?

It’s like the stars in the big dipper exist, but the big dipper only came into being when someone gazed into the heavens and out of a gazillion stars to choose from, selected those eight, decided they kind of looked like a punch bowl implement and sketched out a picture on the side of their cave.

The Big Dipper

The big dipper didn’t always exist. It was born the moment someone noticed it and decided it was a thing. The Law of Gravity didn’t always exist, it was born the moment Newton conceived of it. On one hand we have the label for what we think we understand. On the other, we have the set of phenomena we toted together to build that understanding. Let’s not confuse the two.

This idea underpins half this book. I’ve been thinking about it for a month now.
