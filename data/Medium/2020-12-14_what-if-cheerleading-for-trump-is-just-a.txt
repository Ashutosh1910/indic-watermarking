What if cheerleading for Trump is just an effort to stave off a great shame?

Petr Swedock

I’ve never told another soul — I’m ashamed, but it’s true — about the time I got taken by a con man.

It was in the mid-to-late 1980’s, in Boston. I was hanging out, waiting for a show at a small local jazz club to start. The show, however, was limited to ages 21+. I was not yet 21 but in those days, it was sometimes easier to slip in unnoticed. If I got in and didn’t order any drinks I might be ok. This is important to the story.

It was the late afternoon. I was walking near the famous Boston Commons, on Tremont street, just thinking.

A man stopped me. ‘How you doing?’ he asked. Friendly, without being creepy. Somehow he had sized me up, and he did so extraordinarily well.

I told him I was well. ‘How about yourself?’ I asked in return.

‘Good, good. Where you headed?’

I told him there was a show at 1369 Club, to which I was then headed. I don’t recall exactly, but I may have used the word ‘bar.’

‘You need a fake ID? I can get you a fake ID.’ He said. I must have blinked, started, or in some other manner betrayed my surprise, because — as noted — I was not 21, and had been plotting to slip in and enjoy the show. If I had a fake ID it would be so much easier. I could get a beer and fit in even more.

I was hooked. ‘Sure!’ I said.

We went to a small cafe and got a cup of coffee together, and talked for a while. He put me at ease. We did discuss what it would take, then we talked about jazz and sports and about a variety of things. I began to think that I had made a new friend. He asked if I wanted to go through with it. He was giving me an out, but I remember thinking to myself, ‘I’m already in it, might as well go farther.’ I gave him what I thought was a fair amount of money for a fake ID. He then asked if I wanted some marijuana. I didn’t, particularly, but I knew some people who might and I thought I could resell it to them, limit my losses, so to speak. So I gave him some more money. He left, promising to return with a fake ID and a bag of marijuana.

I waited. And I waited. I had another cup of coffee. I missed the show. Still I waited. He never returned. Of course he didn’t.

I still remember the sting and impact I felt when the slow, creeping realization of hot shame met the chilling streak of cold sweat running down my back. At first, slowly, then all at once, as reality asserted itself and it was awful. I was out plenty of money, but more impactful was the dent in my pride and self-image. I was crushed.

I spent the next few weeks in a blue funk, looking for arguments and fights, tight with a clenched, bitter, self-recrimination and shame.
