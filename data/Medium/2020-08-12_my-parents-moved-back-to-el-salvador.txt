My parents moved back to El Salvador

Ana Rosa Granados

After both my sister and I graduated from college, my mom and dad finally sat us down and told us that their work here in the U.S is done. We then looked at each other in the eyes and realized that this has been something we knew was going to happen in time but didn’t truly understand the reasons why until we got older.

My parents had no choice but to move to the U.S when a violent civil war broke out in El Salvador in the 1980s, this was a long time coming. So, they did what many Salvadorean immigrants had to do to survive, they went on a life-threatening voyage to the United States. They barely made it to the U.S and worked very hard, provided a better future for their family and now they are back in their country trying to reconnect to it. Some never truly make it back or feel reconnected to their country or ancestors.

My father mainly fled because he organized, fought alongside farmers and students who were against the military government. Many of whom were looking for him after he got to the U.S. He landed in a small New York City apartment in the Bronx where his sister “my tia” was living at the time. I to this day admire him for what he did for his country and never really questioned his decisions. Until, he was about to go back to El Salvador and I asked him why after so many years in the U.S he decided to leave? and this is what he said “Hija El Salvador es mi tierra, y todos los dias pienso en ella, en este pais nadie me conoce y nunca me an reconosido ” Which translates to “My Daughter, El Salvador is my homeland and every day I think about her and in this country, no one knows me or has acknowledged who I truly am.”. My father never had the opportunity to connect with others and he was always seen as an outsider, therefore, he felt like one. He also was too busy working endless hours to even have time to think about who he truly was or did in his past.

My mother, on the other hand, came from one of the most rural towns in El Salvador, she was smuggled here in a small car and hiked with other migrants all the way to Jamaica Queens, New York, just to find that her father had a separate family, which was common to Salvadorean culture at the time. She then moved up to the Bronx and met my father. Her trauma is more deep and real. I remember my mother always refused to cook American food and always spoke to me in Spanish even though she taught herself how to speak English, this I am very grateful for. She always struggled to connect with others (non-Spanish folk) because she felt like an outsider all the time but she at times let loose and was so interested in so many other cultures around her.

So, should safety hold them back or fear? I think fear is way behind them and a sense of connection and identity is more important. Now, both my sister and I identify with two countries one that was past and now a present. Another, well is present and kinda erased our past. Culture helped us all and I am glad EL Salvador was a place my parents refused to let go of.
