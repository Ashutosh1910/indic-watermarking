Why didn’t I love the movie Soul?

Kern Carter

A short commentary on the movie Soul.

The trailer to the movie Soul got me excited. Inside Out is one of my favourite Pixar movies and the Soul preview gave me that same kinda vibe. And although the animation kept me in awe the entire film, I thought it missed an opportunity to dive deep into the emotional dangers and social isolation that comes with trying to live your purpose. More plainly, the execution of the story idea was good, not great.

The plot was straightforward enough — their’s meaning in simply being alive and we need to move beyond the belief that we have a singular purpose. Soul tries to show how damaging the purpose narrative can be to our mental health if it’s exchanged for the cost of actually enjoying life.

I got all that, and the movie touched on some heavy topics in a clever way. Its commentary on lost souls is brilliant and was one of the only times my heart and mind were communicating in ways I anticipated would be happening throughout the entire film.

But for me, those moments were rare. I’ve read commentaries that have suggested some racial blunders. Not sure I’d take it that far. The case of one of the souls mistaking Joe, a Black man, with another Black man felt insensitive. And if you really turn up your racial lens, I can maybe see how what could be interpreted as a white woman speaking in the body of a Black man for a large portion of the film can be off-putting. But I didn’t view it that way.

I actually thought that race was incidental to the film and not meant to be considered as part of its theme, at least not a primary one.

For me, I think there was an opportunity to dive deeper into the relationship between the lost souls and the lack of purpose. They teased it well, and at the end of the movie we get glimpses into how that relationship can quickly descend into depression. But a tease is all we got, and instead the plot centred more around what it means to appreciate life.

I also thought the film could’ve had Joe, the main character, interact more with his family and friends on earth. The barbershop scene was a standout moment. When Joe finishes his haircut and asks the barber why he’s never told him about his life, the barber says, “because you never asked.” That’s when Joe first started to realize that there’s more to life than just jazz and that maybe the fixation on his purpose had made him selfish.

The conversation he had with his mother about supporting his passion is another moment that tugs at your heartstrings. I could’ve watched three or four more scenes like that. The relatability of those moments were powerful and gave glimpses into how much more moving this film could’ve been.

That said, this is an animated movie meant for children. My feelings as a mid-thirties viewer should be taken with that preface. And to be honest, I liked the film, I just didn’t love it. I’m curious to see what others have to say about it as more people start sitting with its messages.
