‘Primal’ S1E4 review- Things to fear under a blood moon

Dhinoj Dings

When the blood moon rises, things go seriously wrong. Any self-respecting horror movie fan would know this. And ‘Terror under the blood room’ is the episode with the strongest horror vibe so far.

The episode starts with Spear and Fang, or rather Spear sitting atop Fang, being chased by a flock of velociraptors. And by flock, I mean FLOCK since there are too many raptors to count.

One of the most visually intriguing sequences happens when our heroes flee into what appears to be a dried corn field and then use the field as a hideout to take the raptors by surprise.(My knowledge of the world’s fauna is less than optimal. So, there’s fair chance it’s not a corn field after all.)

But once they come out in to the open, they are once again surrounded by the raptors- who are painted in an appropriately fiery red.

It looks like this is the end of the road for our heroes. But then, dusk falls, and the blood moon rises. The raptors get scared and they scram.

Spear and Fang move ahead in their destination-free journey only to encounter their next foes- fantastical flying beings that look like a cross between a demon from hell and a garden variety bat.

At one point, some of these creatures carry Spear to the lair of a giant spider, perched atop a mountain.

Fang daringly goes in search of Spear.

This is something of a reversal of roles- in one of the previous episodes, we see Spear going out of his way to help Fang escape the ..err…fangs of viciously poisonous snakes.

We humans have the tremendous potential to shape our environments for the better(which is essentially the reversal of what’s happening right now).

The idea that a good turn from Spear now gets reciprocated by Fang seems to remind us of this fact. If we are benevolent to our fellow creatures, we can also expect the same. A karmic rule which mystic traditions extoll.

Also, the idea of good begetting good is here played out by an animal- that too, one we humans understand as the most reptilian.

By virtue of this, Tartakovsky once again asks us the question about what differentiates humans from other animals.

You could argue that the velociraptor pose something of an answer to this question- after all, they relentlessly purse Fang and Spear without anything resembling compassion, something that humans won’t do, right?

But then, when he is hungry, we often see Spear also remorselessly killing innocent animals. Not necessarily because he is a bad human but because that’s how creatures on God’s earth behave- when they are hungry, they do whatever they can to eat.

As ever, the question only seems to get the same answer, over and over again: When it comes right down to it, the difference between humans and animals- in terms of behavior- is negligible.

One might think that arriving at the same answer repeatedly might be redundant.

But the ways in which the filmmakers undertake the exploration that seemingly leads to this answer are never less than thrilling.
