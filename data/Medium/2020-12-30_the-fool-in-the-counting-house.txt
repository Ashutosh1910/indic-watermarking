The Fool in the Counting-House

Matt Boomer

“Come, now,” the portly man replied. “You should know as well as I that there is little to be done for such people; if they cannot change their lot in life why not allow the state to sort them out? We have, after all, already paid to furnish such efforts.”

The plump gentleman leaned back in his chair, adjusting in the seat behind his desk. A moneylender and a trader on the exchanges, he had been an acquaintance of the old man for many years. For a long stretch he may have been by default the old man’s closest friend.

“Do you not read the journals, my friend? Do you not know the numbers of these masses are growing to an extent which cannot be controlled, and which will in time overwhelm any capacity we have to care for them? What do you believe will come of that situation if we attempt to meddle with the natural order of things?”

The old man furrowed his brow, at once flummoxed by the man’s intransigence and overcome with understanding and even sympathy for him; at this time a year ago, nearly these very words had been his. And how often, even in the time since, had he struggled against the temptation to renege on new commitments and fall back into old modes of thought and deed? Life was still hard, the world still cruel and unfeeling, and invitations to despair and cynicism still legion. The siren song of security, tangible in gold and measurable with but a few reams of paper, could always be heard.

Finally, the old man countered:

“It is not for you or me to make final judgments on such things; and though I do not believe our capacities as limited as you do, I do not wish to venture into political questions. It is for me only to use what time and resources have been given me to bless my fellow men, and it is in that cause I hope you will join me. If you would but consider it as a favor to me?”

The portly man stroked his large chin. The old man had changed much, an alteration which no one seemed to be able to explain. He had, seemingly overnight, become prone to a treacly, naive tenderness toward lower specimens of man, given to quixotic endeavors which profited him nothing. But the portly man could not deny that he had adopted a new conviviality, and become far better company.

“For your sake, my friend, I’ll make a contribution. My clerk will let you know what to put me down for; I’ve known this conversation was coming for some time.”

The old man eagerly thanked him, finalized their business and exited, knowing perfectly well that as soon as the door shut behind him his friend would be laughing at him, and in that he would be far from alone. He knew there was great chatter about the fool from the old counting-house — though it looked considerably less old of late — and his newfound crusades, frivolities, and base sentimentality. Let them quip and crack wise; he would take it in stride, remembering how eagerly he once joined them. And he would keep the door open to them as it had been left open to him, and rejoice with them when they entered.
